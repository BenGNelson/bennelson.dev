the homelab

Everything on the dashboard runs on one mid-tower in my basement. This is the machine, and this is what it does all day.

This is production: one mid-tower, built from parts, running 24/7 a few feet from the workbench it was assembled on. It carries the media server, the network storage, the smart-home brain, and every app on the dashboard, all at once, on six cores that rarely notice.

The storage is a stack of SSDs in software RAID. It survives losing a drive, and the stuff I genuinely couldn't replace also leaves the house every night, encrypted, to sleep somewhere else.

the container platform

The apps on the dashboard aren't hosted anywhere. They're containers on this box. Each one is its own small stack with its own compose file and its own network, so a bad day for one app is a boring day for the rest. At any given moment about twenty containers are running.

New code gets built and tested on a laptop upstairs, then shipped down to the basement. Images refresh themselves overnight, so if something breaks at 4am, past me finds out at breakfast.

House rules: the firewall denies by default, versions are pinned, and nothing inside the house listens to the internet. The one thing friends can reach connects outward through a tunnel, so there's no door to knock on.

the house is a client

The same box runs the smart-home brain, with two USB radios plugged into it: one speaks Zigbee, the other Z-Wave. It knows what the thermostats are doing, whether the washer and dryer have finished, how charged the car is, and whether the cat's heated blanket is on. (The blanket has its own smart plug. The cat is the most monitored resident of the house.)

On top of that sits home-hq, the dashboard that started this whole site. The real one never leaves the house, but there's a full simulation with canned data you can click around.

the workbench

The unfinished half of the basement is where hardware goes to be tinkered with. A 3D printer that mostly prints upgrades for itself. A Raspberry Pi that boots straight into a retro game console. A laser printer old enough to have opinions about drivers, resurrected as a network printer by plugging it into the server. And the docking spot where the laptops live between trips around the house.

Every project on the dashboard started on this bench, got built on a laptop, and earned its way onto the machine humming next to it. That's the whole pipeline, and the commute is one flight of stairs.
